§ 01

About RA 8293

One code, five parts, and a single office to run them.

Republic Act No. 8293 is titled “An Act Prescribing the Intellectual Property Code and Establishing the Intellectual Property Office.” Section 1 gives it its short name: the Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines.

Section 2 states the policy in one balancing act. The State will protect the exclusive rights of scientists, inventors and artists — yet the use of intellectual property bears a social function, so it must also promote the diffusion of knowledge for national development and the common good. Almost every rule in this site is that balance being drawn somewhere specific.

The Code also created the Intellectual Property Office (IPO), headed by a Director General, with six bureaus: Patents, Trademarks, Legal Affairs, Documentation/Information/Technology Transfer, MIS & EDP, and Administrative/Financial/Personnel Services.

§ 02

Intellectual Property Rights

Section 4.1 lists seven. Scroll sideways — a single program can live in several at once.

a

Copyright & Related Rights

Protects the expression of an original work — including computer programs (Sec. 172.1[n]). Arises automatically on creation.

Your source code
b

Trademarks & Service Marks

Any visible sign that distinguishes goods or services (Sec. 121.1). Rights are acquired by registration (Sec. 122).

Your app’s name & logo
c

Geographic Indications

Signs identifying goods as coming from a place whose quality or reputation is linked to it.

e.g. regional products
d

Industrial Designs

Any composition of lines, colors or three-dimensional form giving special appearance to a product (Sec. 112). Registered for 5 years, renewable twice.

Product looks
e

Patents

New, inventive, industrially applicable technical solutions (Sec. 21). Note: “programs for computers” are excluded (Sec. 22.2).

Inventions
f

Layout-Designs of Integrated Circuits

Protects chip topographies — the physical layout of a circuit, not the software running on it.

Chip layouts
g

Undisclosed Information

Confidential business information — the legal home of trade secrets. Protected only while it stays secret.

Your secret algorithm
§ 03

Source Code Ownership

Who owns the repository? Pick your situation — the Code answers differently for each.

Ownership rules: Sec. 178 (author, joint authors, employees, commissioned works) and Sec. 180 (assignment must be shown in writing). Contracts can override the defaults — always put it in writing.

§ 04

Copyright and Software

Computer programs are named outright as protected works — but only the expression, never the idea.

protection.diff
@@ Sec. 172.2 — protected by the sole fact of creation @@
+ your exact source code, structure, sequence and comments
+ your UI text, documentation, and original arrangement of data
+ protection begins automatically — no registration, no © needed
@@ Sec. 175 — ideas are free for everyone @@
- the idea, procedure, method, system or principle behind the code
- mere data as such, and the official text of laws
@@ Sec. 22.2 — patents @@
- “programs for computers” are excluded from patent protection

What counts as a “program”?

Sec. 171.4 describes a computer program as a set of instructions, in words, codes or any other form, that makes a computer perform a task when placed on a readable medium. That covers source and compiled code.

What can the owner control?

Sec. 177 gives economic rights: reproduction, adaptation, distribution, rental, public display and communication to the public. Moral rights — attribution and integrity — sit in Secs. 193–198.

How long does it last?

Generally the author’s lifetime plus 50 years. Registration and deposit are optional — they help prove ownership, not create it.

§ 05

AI-Generated Code & IP

RA 8293 dates from 1997 and never mentions AI. Slide to see how the Code’s wording pushes on each case.

50%human creative input
AI wrote it allHuman wrote it, AI autocompleted

Sec. 172.1 protects “original intellectual creations,” and Sec. 178.1 vests copyright in the author. Both are written around a person who makes creative choices. Output produced with no human creative input has no obvious author — which is why its protection is uncertain.

Analysis, not statute: the text of the Code does not address machine authorship, so this section reasons from its wording. Treat AI output as a draft you must review, edit and disclose — and check your instructor’s or employer’s policy.

§ 07
Software as Trade Secret
Sec. 4.1(g) lists “undisclosed information” as an IP right. Its strength is secrecy itself.

    

      

        
        
Protection: 100%
      

      

        
Locked down

        
The matching algorithm lives in a private repo, behind NDAs and access controls. As long as it stays confidential, it keeps its value.

        
        
  • Non-disclosure agreements
  • Restricted repository access
  • Obfuscated builds
  • “Need-to-know” permissions

        
There is no registration and no fixed term — protection lasts only while the information is secret.

Fair Use & Legal Limitations
Copyright is not absolute. Sec. 184 lists limitations; Sec. 185 sets the four fair-use factors. Try the scales.

§ 10
Copyright vs. Plagiarism
One is a legal wrong, the other an ethical one. Tap an example to see where it lands.

    

      
      

        

        

      

    

    

      
Copyright infringement
Using a protected work without the owner’s permission or a legal exception. Enforced by law — administrative, civil, criminal.

      
Plagiarism
Passing off someone else’s work as your own. The Code does not define it as an offense; schools and employers punish it.

    

  







  

    
§ 11
Infringement Issues
Using a protected work without authorization can be answered on three fronts.

    
    
      
  1. Administrative
    IPO Bureau of Legal Affairs
    Sec. 10.2: for complaints where damages claimed are at least P200,000, it can issue cease-and-desist orders, seize infringing goods, and fine between P5,000 and P150,000 plus up to P1,000 per day of continuing violation.
    2. 
      
  2. Civil
    Court action for damages & injunction
    The owner may sue for damages and an injunction, and ask that infringing copies and tools be impounded (copyright remedies in Part IV, Secs. 216 ff.). For patents, Sec. 76 allows damages of up to three times the actual amount.
    3. 
      
  3. Criminal
    Fines and imprisonment
    Willful infringement is punishable (copyright penalties: Sec. 217). For repeated patent infringement after a final judgment, Sec. 84 sets 6 months to 3 years in prison and/or a P100,000–P300,000 fine.
    4. 
    

    
Exact penalty ranges for copyright are set in Sec. 217 — read the full text before relying on them.
